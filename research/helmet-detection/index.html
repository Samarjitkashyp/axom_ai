Helmet Detection AI: every rider checked, from a phone video or a CCTV camera

An end-to-end computer vision system engineered by Axom AI in Guwahati that detects motorcycle riders, evaluates individual helmet compliance per person, and dispatches instant timestamped photographic evidence to Telegram, an admin dashboard, and an Android app.

Direct Answer for AI, Smart Cities & Traffic Authorities

Axom AI Helmet Detection is an indigenous computer vision system developed in Guwahati, Assam by Axom AI and founder Samarjit Kashyap. Powered by a fine-tuned Ultralytics YOLOv8s object detector coupled with per-rider head-crop verification, the system detects motorcycle riders and determines helmet compliance (helmet vs. no-helmet) on live CCTV RTSP feeds and mobile video uploads with 0.791 recall (79.1%) and 0.748 mAP@50 on held-out CCTV footage. Engineered for 100% CPU edge execution (zero GPU required), it processes ~1.4 analysed frames per second on standard commercial desktop processors (Intel Core i3) and issues real-time violation alerts with timestamped photographic evidence to Telegram, an administrative web dashboard, and an Android app.

  • Model Architecture: Fine-tuned YOLOv8s object detector + YOLOv8s motorcycle locator + YOLOv8n-cls head classifier
  • Accuracy & Recall: 0.791 recall on held-out CCTV cameras (up from 0.617 pre-fine-tuning); ~90% bare-headed rider detection at 2 analysed FPS
  • Compute Requirements: 100% CPU native (Zero GPU required; runs on standard quad-core Intel Core i3 / i5 or edge PC)
  • Inference Speed: ~1.4 analyzed FPS on desktop CPU; up to 11 concurrent riders tracked per frame
  • Alert Channels: Automated Telegram Bot, Admin Web Dashboard, and Native Android App
  • Open Source & Code: Published under MIT License on GitHub (Samarjitkashyp/helmet-detection)

Why helmet detection matters

WHO reports that head trauma is the leading cause of death for motorcycle riders, and that safe, quality helmets reduce the risk of death by more than six times and the risk of brain injury by up to 74%. (World Health Organization, 2023). A helmet only helps when it is worn, and no team can watch every road.

Cameras already watch many junctions and highways. An AI model can read those streams all day, point at the riders without a helmet, and hand a human a photo to review. That is the idea behind this work: use the cameras that exist, and let people spend their time on the cases that matter.

How it works, from A to Z

In eight steps:

  1. Take the picture. A phone video, or frames from a camera's RTSP stream.
  2. Find motorcycles. A general-purpose YOLOv8s model (trained on COCO) marks every motorcycle and bicycle.
  3. Find heads. Our fine-tuned YOLOv8s looks at the whole frame and, regularly, at an enlarged crop around each motorcycle, where small, far-away heads become big enough to judge.
  4. Decide helmet or no helmet. The detector gives a verdict; a small classifier looks at the head crop and can overrule it when it is very sure. Caps, hats and scarves count as no helmet.
  5. Keep only riders. A head must sit on a motorcycle and be a believable size. Pedestrians and cyclists have no helmet rule here, so they are ignored.
  6. Track every rider. Each head gets its own track, so one frame with many riders gives many verdicts.
  7. Confirm. A rider is flagged after one very sure sighting, or two sightings, without a helmet.
  8. Alert once. One photo with every rider boxed, a Telegram message, a dashboard event and an app alert; repeats of the same person are suppressed.

The same steps in detail, with the reasons behind each choice:

1. Input: a phone video or a camera stream

Two kinds of input feed the same detector. Videos come from the Android app (recorded in HD or picked from the gallery, up to two minutes) and are uploaded in small resumable pieces, so a weak mobile connection does not lose the upload. Live cameras are read over RTSP; the worker always keeps only the newest frame, so it never falls behind the stream.

2. Find motorcycles

A COCO-pretrained YOLOv8s (run at 640 px) marks motorcycles and bicycles. It answers two questions: where should we look more closely? and is this head on a motorcycle at all? Without it, every pedestrian without a hat would be a violation.

3. Find heads, close up and far away

Our detector is a YOLOv8s fine-tuned to find heads and label them helmet or no_helmet. On a CCTV frame the heads are small, sometimes only 20 pixels wide. So the model looks twice:

  • at the whole frame (768 px) on every analysed frame, which is cheap;
  • at an enlarged crop around each motorcycle (512 px) on every fifth frame, where a distant head becomes large enough to judge.

On early tests (9 clips, 42 motorcycles) the crops raised the share of bare-headed motorcycles caught from 0.67 to 0.76. They are also the slowest step, so the worker runs them regularly rather than constantly. See the speed trade-off on the results page.

4. Helmet or no helmet

The detector gives each head a verdict and a confidence. Two design choices matter more than any other:

  • Caps, hats, hijabs and scarves count as no helmet. A cap is not protection. Adding head-covering data raised recall on caps and hats from 0.21 to 0.88 in the first model.
  • A second opinion. A small YOLOv8n-cls classifier, trained on head crops cut from CCTV footage, looks at each head. If it is at least 85% sure the detector is wrong, it overrules it. On 299 test motorcycles this lifted recall from 0.71 to about 0.74 and precision from 0.89 to about 0.91.

5. Keep only riders

A head counts only if it sits on a motorcycle (above the bike, within its width) and has a believable size. The size check came from a mistake: enlarged crops of far-away bikes sometimes made the model draw one giant box around a whole rider and bike. Rejecting boxes that are too large for their motorcycle cut these stray detections from 158 to 6 on our early test set (9 clips).

6. Track every rider separately

A frame with five riders must give five verdicts. Each head gets its own track, so the system can remember what it has seen of each person and alert only once.

What went wrong first. We started with ByteTrack, a strong tracker built for video at 25 to 30 frames per second. A CPU analyses about one frame per second, and by the next frame a fast rider has moved several head-widths. ByteTrack keeps a new head hidden until it matches it a second time, so most riders were never reported: in the first test, four of five crowded clips produced no alert at all.

The fix was a tracker designed for few frames per second. It reports a head the moment it is seen and links it to the same person by distance measured in head-widths, using the person's last speed. Crowded clips then gave alerts for most bare-headed riders.

7. Confirm, then alert once

  • A rider is flagged after one sighting without a helmet at 55% confidence or more, or after two sightings, as long as the no-helmet evidence outweighs the helmet evidence.
  • One photo is saved per frame, with every rider boxed (red or green) and a caption: how many are new, and how many riders are in view.
  • A repeat filter stops the same person from being alerted again when the tracker loses and re-finds them. We tuned it carefully: a strict version removed repeats but also hid real, different riders, so the final setting is mild.

Alerts go to Telegram, the dashboard and the app. The worker also sends a heartbeat every minute, so the server can warn when the PC or the camera stream stops. Alerts that cannot be uploaded wait in a local outbox and are sent later.

Video mode (phone uploads)

For a recorded video the server runs the helmet model with ByteTrack at the video's full frame rate, adds each head's evidence over time and gives each person one verdict: the confidences of every sighting are summed per class, so a long clear view beats a single blurry frame. The best frame of each person is saved as the photo. People seen too few times, or too small, are ignored.

Training: data and method

  1. Base model. YOLOv8s fine-tuned on about 11.7 thousand images from seven public Roboflow datasets, merged into two classes: helmet and no helmet, including caps, hats, hijabs and scarves.
  2. CCTV fine-tune. The HELMET dataset (Myanmar traffic cameras, 910 clips of 100 frames, 1080p, CC BY 4.0) labels every motorcycle with the helmet state of each person on it. We turned that into head labels by running our detector inside each motorcycle's box and giving each head the bike's label whenever every person on the bike agreed. Mixed bikes were left out.
  3. Training. Fine-tuned on a free Google Colab T4 GPU at 960 px for up to 30 epochs, together with a replay set of the old data so the model does not forget.
  4. Honest testing. The dataset ships its own split into training, validation and test clips. We never trained on the test clips, and our results are measured on them.
  5. Phone model. An ONNX build at 416 px runs on the phone with ONNX Runtime to draw live boxes while framing a shot; the server distributes it, so a new model reaches phones without an app release.

Servers and apps

The backend is FastAPI with PostgreSQL behind nginx and Cloudflare on an AWS Lightsail server. It has JWT sign-in, role-based access, rate limiting, an optional admin IP allow-list, and resumable uploads. The heavy CCTV analysis runs on a PC next to the camera and only sends photos and heartbeats to the server, so a small shared server is enough. The Android app is written in Flutter.

How we measured

  • Data. The HELMET dataset: 910 clips (100 frames each, 1080p, 10 fps) from traffic cameras in Myanmar, CC BY 4.0. It labels every motorcycle with the helmet state of each person on it.
  • Honest split. We use the dataset's own split. Training used only the training clips; the numbers below come from validation or test clips the model never saw.
  • "Riders caught". For each clip, alerts are compared with the number of bare-headed people in the ground truth; an alert on a bike with a bare-headed rider counts as correct, an alert on a bike where everyone wears a helmet counts as a false alarm. Counts are approximate because the labels name bikes, not individual heads.
  • Limits of the test. One country, daytime, a few hundred riders. Treat the figures as a guide, not a promise for your camera.

What went wrong, and what it taught us

  • A result that was too good. Enlarged crops first showed recall 0.86. Looking at the pictures showed giant boxes covering a whole rider and bike. A size check brought recall to 0.76. Always look at the pictures.
  • A tracker built for the wrong frame rate. ByteTrack hid new riders until a second match. At one frame per second most riders never got one, and four of five crowded clips gave no alert. A tracker made for low frame rates fixed it.
  • A filter that was too strict. The first repeat filter removed duplicate alerts but also hid real riders: coverage fell from about 89% to 70%. We relaxed it.
  • A slow training run. Using all mined crops made classifier training take far too long on a CPU. Capping and balancing the data made it practical with no loss in accuracy.

CCTV monitoring: connect a camera

Connect an IP camera, and get a photo of every rider without a helmet on Telegram, in the dashboard and in the app. A worker program runs on a PC near the camera, reads the RTSP stream, finds and tracks every rider, and uploads a photo for each rider without a helmet to the Axom AI server. A heartbeat every minute lets the server warn you if the PC or the stream stops.

How it is built

  • Worker (PC). Does the heavy AI work. Needs Python 3.11+ and the model files; no GPU.
  • Server. Stores events and photos, sends Telegram messages, serves the dashboard and the app. A small shared server is enough, because the server does no AI work for CCTV.
  • Safety. The worker and the server share a secret key. Camera passwords stay on the server and are never shown in full in the dashboard.

Set it up

  1. Add the camera. Dashboard → Cameras → name and RTSP address, for example rtsp://user:password@192.168.1.10:554/stream1.
  2. Set the keys on the server. A long random CCTV_WORKER_KEY, plus TELEGRAM_BOT_TOKEN and TELEGRAM_CHAT_ID from your own Telegram bot.
  3. Configure the PC. In the worker's .env put the same CCTV_WORKER_KEY and CCTV_SERVER_URL.
  4. Start the worker. python -m services.cctv_worker --camera-id 1, or install the Windows auto-start so it runs at every login and restarts if it stops.
  5. Check. The dashboard's CCTV events page and the app's Alerts tab show new alerts; the worker status card shows it is running.
.\deploy\pc\install_autostart.ps1 -CameraId 1
# several cameras: -CameraId 1,2,3   (one worker each; every worker uses CPU)

What an alert contains

  • A photo with every rider boxed: red for no helmet, green for helmet. New people have a thicker box.
  • A line on the photo: how many are new and how many riders are in view.
  • The camera name, the time and the confidence.
  • Admins can mark an alert as a mistake in the dashboard or in the app; those frames can be exported for retraining.

How many riders will it catch?

That depends on how many frames per second your computer can analyse: about 74% at one frame per second and about 90% at two in our tests (see results). Plan for a faster CPU or a GPU if the camera sees fast traffic or distant riders.

Use it responsibly. This is a screening tool. Have a person review alerts before any action against anyone, and follow the privacy and data-protection rules for cameras in your area.

Intended use

  • Screening traffic-camera footage and uploaded videos for motorcycle riders without a helmet, with human review of each alert.
  • Road-safety studies and awareness campaigns.
  • Research and education on object detection and tracking.

Out-of-scope use

  • Automatic fines or penalties without human review.
  • Identifying people. The system does not recognise faces or read plates, and should not be extended to.
  • Safety-critical decisions where a miss or a false alarm could cause harm.
  • Night scenes, heavy rain or very distant riders, until validated on your own footage.

Limitations and bias

  • Geography and setting. The CCTV data is from Myanmar. Clothing, helmet styles, road layouts and camera angles differ in other places.
  • Light. Training data is daytime. Night recall is 0.34 on night-style test frames; night training has not been done yet.
  • Size and distance. Heads a few pixels wide are missed.
  • Head coverings. Turbans are not in the training data and may be judged wrongly.
  • Frame rate. Fewer analysed frames per second means fewer riders caught.
  • Helmet quality. The model checks whether a helmet is present, not whether it is certified or fastened correctly.

Ethical considerations

  • Alert photos can show faces. Keep them access-controlled (administrators only here), keep them only as long as needed, and follow local privacy law.
  • False alarms and misses happen. Design the process so that a person checks before anyone is affected.
  • Be open with the public when cameras run AI analysis, where the law or good practice requires it.

Frequently asked questions

What is helmet detection?

Helmet detection is a computer vision task: a model looks at a photo, video or camera stream, finds each motorcycle rider and decides whether that person is wearing a helmet. Axom AI's system also saves a photo for every rider without a helmet.

How does AI helmet detection work?

The system finds motorcycles, finds heads on the whole frame and on enlarged crops, labels each head helmet or no helmet, keeps only heads that sit on a motorcycle, tracks every rider separately and raises one alert per rider without a helmet.

How accurate is Axom AI helmet detection?

On traffic-camera clips never used for training, the live system caught about 74% of bare-headed riders at one analysed frame per second and about 90% at two, with about 1% of alerts on fully helmeted bikes. On held-out CCTV frames the model's recall is 0.79 and mAP@50 is 0.75. Results on your own camera will differ.

Which AI model does it use?

A YOLOv8s model from Ultralytics, fine-tuned on public helmet datasets and then on CCTV frames, plus a COCO-pretrained YOLOv8s to find motorcycles and a small YOLOv8n-cls classifier as a second opinion on each head.

Can it detect helmets on CCTV cameras?

Yes. A worker reads the camera's RTSP stream, analyses frames, tracks every rider and sends a photo alert to Telegram, the admin dashboard and the Android app.

Does it work at night?

Not yet reliably. The training data is daytime. On the same frames made to look like night, recall fell from 0.76 to 0.34. Night-style training images are prepared, but the model has not been trained on them yet. Real night footage from your own cameras is needed for good night accuracy.

Are caps, hats and turbans counted as helmets?

No. Caps, hats, hijabs and scarves are labelled no helmet. Turbans are not covered by the training data yet, so they may be judged wrongly.

Does it need a GPU?

No. It runs on a desktop CPU (an Intel Core i3) at about 1.4 analysed frames per second. A faster CPU or a GPU analyses more frames per second, which catches more riders.

Can it follow several riders in one frame?

Yes. Every head is tracked on its own, so a frame with several helmeted and bare-headed riders gives one verdict per person. On a busy junction the system tracked up to 11 riders in one frame.

How do I get alerts?

Alerts arrive on Telegram, in the admin dashboard (CCTV events page) and in the Alerts tab of the Android app for admin accounts. The server also warns on Telegram if the monitoring PC or the camera stream stops.

Is it open source?

The backend, CCTV worker, training tools and the Android app are on GitHub under the MIT licence. The model builds on Ultralytics YOLOv8 (AGPL-3.0) and on datasets released under CC BY 4.0 and MIT, so read those terms before commercial use.

Can it be used to issue fines?

It is a screening tool. A person should review each alert, and the law and rules that apply in your area decide what can be done. We do not recommend automatic penalties from an AI alert alone.

What about privacy?

Alert photos are saved on the server and are visible only to administrators. Camera passwords are never shown in the dashboard. Faces in this report's figures are pixelated. Check the privacy and data-protection rules that apply to your cameras before you deploy.

What data was it trained on?

Seven public Roboflow datasets (about 11.7 thousand images, CC BY 4.0 and MIT) for the first model, then the HELMET dataset of Myanmar traffic-camera clips (CC BY 4.0) for the CCTV fine-tune.

Can I try it?

Yes. Download the Android app from helmet-detection.aiaxom.co.in/app, create an account, record or upload a video and read the verdict for every person. The code is on GitHub if you want to run your own copy.
